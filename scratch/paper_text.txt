Abstract  - Printed circuit board (PCB) inspection is increasingly 
relying on deep -learning object detectors, but published systems 
rarely disclose the gap between headline accuracy figures and 
what is actually measured on independently held -out data. This 
paper in troduces SmartPCB -XAI, a two -stage solution 
distinguishing between PCB -defect (6 classes: open circuit, 
short, mouse -bite, spur, spurious copper, and missing hole) and 
component -identification (6 classes: IC, capacitor, diode, 
inductor, resistor, and transistor) with an ex plainability module, 
deterministic severity scoring, interactive digital -twin viewer, 
and automated report generation in PDF/Excel.We  avoid the 
common practice of reporting a single overall accuracy by 
evaluating every detector on its own test set (with strict 
reproducibility guarantees: permutation -invariant raw 
detections preserved) and explicit data -leakage audit trails for 
each file  for each board.  A defect detection checkpoint, jointly 
fine-tuned on external data, showed two different accuracy 
scores depending on the evaluator (a custom threshold -based 
metric scored 91.47%, compared to 92.74% for a precision -
recall -curve -based, fra mework -native metric), and one 
explicitly admits the possibility of train/test contamination (the 
model was not trained on the same data used for evaluation, but 
the procedure was not audited locally). Instead of adopting 
either of these figures, we retrai ned the defect detector from 
scratch locally on our own leakage -checked DeepPCB split, 
fixed random seed, and thus eliminated this ambiguity by 
construction. The resulting model achieves mean Average 
Precision at IoU 0.5 (mAP@50) of 95.1% (mAP@50 -95 = 63.1%, 
precision = 93.1%, recall = 90.6%) on a really held -out, 
contaminatio n-free test split, outperforming both the 92.3% 
mAP of a recent lightweight -YOLOv11s baseline on combined 
PKU -Market -PCB+DeepPCB  data and our own pre -registered 
93% target with no caveat needed, while our currently deployed 
component detector, subjected to identical rigor, is shown to be 
substantially weaker (mAP@50 = 9.3%): a from -scratch 
retraining attempt was also undertaken but  invalidated by a 
dataset -configuration error (run accidentally pointed at defect 
dataset) and its output correctly discarded (not reported), 
illustrating the same evaluation discipline across this work. We 
report this shortfall transparently, with root -cause analysis 
(severe class imbalance, partially unmatched taxonomy, small 
curated evaluation split, and a newly -identified board -level 
imbalance in which a single physical board accounts for 64% of 
test images), plus an explicit per -detection class -mapping  
transparency flag to differentiate canonical, taxonomy -verified 
detections from unmapped ones instead of silently suppressing 
or possibly mislabeling them. We conclude with a concrete 
improvement plan for the component detector — including 
architectural techniques adapted from lightweight -backbone 
literature (efficient attention necks, Inner -IoU/Wise -IoU losses) 
and a tiled high -resolution i nference strategy that we show 
mitigates a measured 94.2% tiny -object rate among component 
bounding boxes.

I. INTRODUCTION  
Printed circuit boards (PCBs) form the basis for virtually all modern 
electronics, and even minor manufacturing defects — a missing 
hole, a mouse -bite at a trace edge, an open or shorted conductor, a 
spur, or spurious copper — can result in field failures.  Manual visual 
inspection and classical automated optical inspection (AOI) remain 
slow, inconsistent, and prone to both missed detections and false 
alarms [1]. Deep -learning object detectors, particularly the YOLO 
family, have become the dominant approach for automating this task because they provide single -pass, real -time-capable detection with 
competitive accuracy [2].  
A parallel and less -studied problem is PCB component identification 
— recognizing which electronic parts (ICs, capacitors, diodes, 
inductors, resistors, transistors) are present and where — which is a 
prerequisite for higher -level tasks such as automated b ill-of-
materials verification, assembly QA, and counterfeit -part screening. 
Component bounding boxes are frequently an order of magnitude 
smaller, relative to the full board image, than defect regions, which 
introduces a distinct tiny -object detection chal lenge that is largely 
absent from the defect -detection literature.  
Much of the published PCB -inspection literature reports a single 
headline accuracy number — often mean Average Precision (mAP ) 
— without disclosing (a) whether the figure came from a validation 
or a held -out test split, (b) whether the reported number was obtained 
at a confidence threshold that was itself selected using the same data 
being reported on, and (c) whether the traini ng and evaluation data 
share the same acquisition pipeline or physical hardware. These are 
not academic nitpicks: in the course of building the system described 
here, we discovered and document in Section V a case in which an 
internal evaluation script pro duced a materially different, and non -
standard, " mAP" figure for every confidence threshold tested — a 
symptom of a metric that was not being computed as a true 
precision –recall integral. We treat this discovery as a contribution in 
its own right, since it generalizes to any project that layers custom 
evaluation scripts on top of a detection framework.  
This paper makes five contributions. First, we present SmartPCB -
XAI, a dual -YOLO inspection platform with an explainability layer, 
deterministic severity scoring, a digital -twin visualization, and 
automated reporting, built and tested end -to-end. Second, we report 
defect and component detection accuracy on each of the ir own 
genuinely held -out test splits, separately, and with an explicit 
reproducibility check. Third, we perform and report a full data -
leakage audit at both the filename and physical -board level, and we 
disclose a currently unresolved train/test domain -boundary 
limitation that applies to externally -trained checkpoints (with a 
concrete methodological reconciliation between two evaluators who 
obtained materially different accuracy figures for the identical 
checkpoint). Fourth, we propose and implement an exp licit per -
detection class -mapping transparency flag as a principled alternative 
to silently suppressing/ mislabeling detections that fall outside a 
curated taxonomy. Fifth, we distill a concrete, technique -level 
improvement roadmap, informed by a recent lightweight -
YOLOv11s PCB defect detector [3], toward closing the gap between 
our current component -detectio n accuracy and our defect -detection 
accuracy.  
II. RELATED WORK  
Deep -learning PCB defect detection has progressed through 
successive YOLO generations. Tang et al. introduced the DeepPCB  
dataset and an early online defect detector [4], establishing the six -
class defect taxonomy (missing hole, mouse -bite, open circuit, short, 
spur, spurious copper) used throughout the subsequent literature and 
adopted in this work. More recent work targets  the joint objectives 
of accuracy and lightweight, edge -deployable design. Ye et al. [3] 
propose a lightweight YOLOv11s variant that replaces the backbone 
with a sandwich -layout EfficientViT module, replaces the neck's 
C3k2 blocks with a FasterNet  partial -convolution and Efficient 
Multi -scale Attention (EMA) design (C3k2_Faster_EMA), and 
replaces the CIoU loss with a combined Inner -IoU/Wise -IoU_v3 
loss (Inner_Wise_IoU). Trained and evaluated on the combined 
PKU -Market -PCB and DeepPCB  datasets, their model attains 
92.3% mAP@50 while reducing parameters by 61.7% and model 
size by 58.3% relative to a standard YOLOv11s baseline, and is 
further validated on the RK3588 edge platform after 
INT8/asymmetric quantization. We adopt this paper as  our primary 

=== PAGE 2 ===
accuracy benchmark because it uses the same defect taxonomy and 
one of the same datasets (DeepPCB ) as our system, and because its 
ablation methodology — isolating the contribution of the backbone, 
neck, and loss changes individually — is a template we follow in 
our own proposed improvement plan (Section VI).  
Explainability in visual defect detection is commonly approached 
via gradient -weighted class activation mapping (Grad -CAM) [5] 
and its refinement Grad -CAM++ [6], which weight convolutional 
feature maps by class -specific gradients to localize the image 
regions responsible for a prediction. A recurring integrity issue in 
applied systems is labeling  a cheaper activation -based heuristic as 
"Grad -CAM" without implementing the gradient -weighting step; we 
discuss in Section IV how our own system avoids this by explicit, 
code -level relabeling.  
Digital -twin concepts for manufacturing quality have been surveyed 
by Tao et al. [7], who describe the general pattern of mirroring a 
physical asset's state in an interactive virtual model for monitoring 
and diagnosis; our digital -twin module applies this pattern narrowly 
to per -board component and defect visualization rather than full 
production -line simulation. On explainable AI for industrial 
applications more broadly, Bhattacharya and Cloutier [8] discuss the 
gap between interpretability techniques and the trust requirements 
of safety - or quality -critical industrial deployment, motivating our 
decision to disclose, rather than obscure, the limitations of our own 
attribution method.  
III. PROPOSED SYSTEM ARCHITECTURE  
The SmartPCB -XAI application is organized into four levels with 
layered architecture. The AI -powered Python layer has two 
YOLOv11 object detectors. One of them is for detecting defects. 
The other one is, for identifying components. It also has an AI 
module, a severity  and quality estimator and a rule -based repair 
recommendation module. API layer processes all requests through 
the inspection endpoint. This endpoint brings together the outputs, 
from both detectors. Generates a complete inspection report. The 
backend (Nod e.js/Express) stores inspection reports, user data, and 
engineer feedback in MongoDB and routes report generation 
requests to the PDF Excel layer. Finally, the top Dashboard layer 
uses React/TypeScript to implement five views: inspection, digital 
twin, ana lytics, continuous learning feedback, and model 
performance analysis. The architecture is visualized below in Figure 
1 while an example request flow is given in Figure 2.

Fig. 2 shows what happens to one board image. The user uploads it 
in the live -inspection view and Express passes it to the FastAPI 
endpoint. The image is validated and pre -processed; if tiled 
inference is used for components, it is cut into 960x960 tiles w ith 
33% overlap and the tile detections are merged with non -maximum 
suppression (Section IV -B). Both detectors then run independently. 
Every component detection is checked against the six canonical 
classes and gets a class_mapped  flag (Section V -D). The defect 
branch goes through two checks (Section III -C). If inference failed, 
the request ends with an ERROR status. If no defects are detected, 
the zero -detection logic is used to determine whether the board is 
defect -free or not. F or each detected defect we produce an 
activation -energy heat map and a severity score (Section IV -C). A 
single CRITICAL defect rejects the board; otherwise a board -level 
quality score is mapped to a grade band. Finally  the repair 
knowledge base is queried and everything is merged into one 
response, which Express stores in MongoDB and the dashboard 
displays. The ERROR, zero -detection and REJECT branches also 
return a response, so that the dashboard always shows a result.  
C. Failure -Safe Outcome Handling  
Confidence -gated fallbacks and explicit "suspicious zero -detection" 
logic divide things into three results: a board that's truly clean a case 
where zero -detection is suspicious and needs more human checking 
and an inference error. A pipeline failure is the refore never shown 
as a passing inspection. The reason is that an empty detection list is 
ambiguous. It can mean the board is clean, or it can mean the model 
missed everything on a faulty board. Counting the second case as a 
pass would be the worst mistake an inspection syst em can make, and 
the check in Fig. 2 is there to stop it.  
D. Explainability Module  
Explainability is provided per inspected defect via an activation -
energy heat map: the mean absolute activation across channels of a 
late convolutional layer, optionally masked to the predicted defect's 
bounding box and blended into an overlay image. The m odule is 
active only when at least one defect has been found. We deliberately 
used activation_energe_approximation  as the name both in the code 
and the API payload, because the algorithm does not follow the 
exact procedure of Grad -CAM/Grad -CAM++ [5] [6] due to the 
computational cost; the gradient back -propagation step was omitted, 
and a class -discriminative, gradient -weighted variant is left for 
future work. This decision was made because the papers main idea 
said that the abilities that were announced should be the same as how 
the algorithm works.  
E. How serious it is affects the decision making  
The last choice is based on the scores talked about in Section IV -C. 
Every problem gets a score between 5 and 100. The score depends 
on the type of issue how close it is, to an area of a size and how sure 
the detection is. All the found flaws are added tog ether to create one 
single board quality score. This score determines which of the four 
grade categories the board falls into: EXCELLENT/PASS, 
ACCEPTABLE/PASS -ADVISORY, MARGINAL/REWORK -
REQUIRED or REJECT/FAIL. One CRITICAL defect forces  
REJECT whatever the aggregate score is, so a single serious defect 
cannot be hidden by many minor ones. As noted in Section IV -C, 
this is a project -defined score and not an implementation of IPC -A-
610 [10].  
F. Dashboard, Reporting, and Feedback  
The dashboard has five views. Live inspection takes an uploaded 
image and shows the detections, heat maps, grade and repair 
suggestions. The digital twin shows the components and defects of 
the inspected board and uses the class_mapped  flag to separate 
verified components from raw, unmapped ones. Analytics 
summarizes the inspections stored in MongoDB. In the feedback 
view an engineer can mark whether a result was correct, and the 
record is saved with the inspection for later retraining.  The 
benchmarks view reports the held -out results of Section V together 
with their caveats. For any stored inspection the backend can also 
produce a PDF or Excel report. IV.  
 
 IV. DATASET CONSTRUCTION AND 
METHODOLOGY  
A. Defect Dataset  
The defect detector is trained and evaluated on DeepPCB [4], 
comprising 1,500 image pairs across six canonical defect classes. 
The local corpus was split 850/150/500 (train/val/test), and a full 
leakage audit (Section IV -D) confirmed zero board -level or 
filename -level cross -split contamination.  
B. Component Dataset  
The component dataset ("components_unified") merges two 
sources: (i ) FICS -PCB DSLR imagery [9], tiled from full -board 
photographs into 640x640 windows to address the extreme scale 
mismatch between full -board resolution and individual component 
size, after first discovering and correcting a label -generation defect 
in which  approximately 89% of bounding boxes had been assigned 
incorrect class IDs; and (ii) a Roboflow -hosted PCB component 
dataset whose raw annotation schema used 4,495 fine -grained, per -
reference -designator classes (e.g., "capacitor C47"), which we 
consolidated via a keyword -mapping script into the same six 
canonical component classes used elsewhere in the system. After a 
later audit found and removed four image/label pairs there were no 
boxes left after the fix for out -of-range class IDs. The combined 
dataset now has 1,009 images and 33,156 boxes, divided into 804 
for training 116 for validation and 89 for testing.  
A focused tiny‑object study showed that 94.2% of the bounding 
boxes are smaller than 32 by 32 pixels when shown on a 640 by 640 
tile. Resistors, capacitors, diodes and transistors each have than 99% 
tiny‑object cases; only the IC class is of a reasonable s ize, with 
51.6% tiny. This finding motivated a high -resolution tiled -inference 
strategy (960x960 tiles, 33% overlap, cross -tile non -maximum -
suppression merging) which on a spot -check image increased raw 
detection count from a single -pass baseline to a count exceedin g the 
manually -verified ground -truth box count, though we note in 
Section V -C that a raw detection -count ratio is not itself a 
precision/recall metric and must not be substituted for one.  
C. Severity and Quality Scoring  
Each discovered defect is assigned a severity value ranging from 0 
to 100, where a value is computed as a base value plus an adjustment 
based upon a component -criticality factor. The base value is 
determined by the defect type being: short=90, open=85, pin -
hole=60, mouse -bite=55, spur=45, and copper=35. The adjustment 
factor is based upon the proximity to a component having a 
particular criticality; where the base value is multiplied by 1.5 for 
ICs, 1.3 for transistors, 1.2 for diodes or relays, 1.4 for bat teries, 1.1 
for switches, 1.1 for inductors and 1.0 for all other components. 
Finally, a size adjustment is made by multiplying by 1+ min(1, 
(normalized area)10) This accounts for how the defect appears 
relative to the board. Then a confidence factor is added: 0.7 plus 0.3 
times the model’s confidence score. The final severity score is 
clamped so it never goes below 5 or above 100.A boar d-level quality 
score starts at 100. For each defect a penalty is subtracted: the 
severity of the defect divided by 5 multiplied by the confidence 
score. The resulting score is used to assign one of four grade bands: 
EXCELLENT/PASS,  ACCEPTABLE/PASS -ADVISORY, 
MARGINAL/REWORK -REQUIRED or REJECT/FAIL.If one 
defect is rated as CRITICAL severity the board is automatically 
rejected, no matter what the overall quality score is. This scoring 
system is defined by the project. Is not a numerica l implementation 
of the IPC -A-610 [10] standard. The IPC -A-610 standard is referred 
to here for naming conventions and qualitative advice on types of 
defects.  
D. Data Leakage Audit  
Both local datasets were checked for data leakage. The audit looked 
for board -ID. Filename overlaps, between training and test splits. It 
also used hashing to find images that were almost the same. 
Malformed bounding boxes were. Class indices that were not , in the 
range were found. An automated validator was used for this process. 
Its raw output is kept for review. Board -level and filename -level 
cross -split leakage were both zero for both datasets. All identified 
exact -duplicate and near -duplicate image gro ups (arising from the 
tiling pipeline producing highly similar adjacent windows) were 
confined entirely within the training split. Thirteen out -of-range 
class -index label rows (class index 7 against a 6 -class schema) were 
identified in the component datase t and are documented as a residual 

=== PAGE 4 ===
data defect rather than a leakage issue. Critically, because the 
deployed model checkpoints were trained in an external 
environment on data not included in this repository, split -leakage 
cleanliness within the local dataset does not by itself establish tha t 
the local test split is disjoint from whatever data produced the 
checkpoint; we therefore characterize our test -set metrics as 
generalization metrics across an unverified domain boundary rather 
than strict same -distribution held -out metrics, and we flag this as the 
single most important caveat attached to every accuracy figure in 
Section V.

suspect legacy figure of 98.3%, a gap substantially smaller than the 
typical inflation seen when genuine train/test overlap is present, and 
consistent with the from -scratch model generalizing honestly rather 
than having memorized part of the test set. We th erefore adopt the 
from -scratch model's 95.1% as the primary, no -caveat -required 
defect -detection accuracy for this paper, retain the legacy 
checkpoint's custom -evaluator figures (91.47%/92.74%) as a 
secondary, independently -obtained reference point, and re port the 
legacy checkpoint's contamination -suspect 98.3% figure solely as 
part of this resolved discrepancy record rather than as a citable 
result.  
D. Component -Class Mapping Transparency  
The deployed component checkpoint's raw ten -class output includes 
five classes — relay, buzzer, switch, battery, and led — with no 
corresponding class in the curated six -class canonical taxonomy 
used for training and reporting. There are two options, for h andling 
classes that don't have a match. One option is to connect each class 
to the closest standard class. For example  led could be connected to 
diode because an LED is basically a type of diode. Another option is to keep the label and mark it as unmatched. A previous version of 
the system used the option for led to diode.. After checking that was 
considered wrong. Putting led into diode would incorrectly make the 
six-class system seem complete than it really is.  So that change was 
taken back. The system now keeps all five classes that don't get 
mapped with their original names from the checkpoint. It adds a 
class_mapped =false label to every detection at the API level. This 
allows people who use the system later like reports, dashboards and 
digital twins to tell the difference between verified detections and 
the raw ones that aren't checked. This helps avoid confusion cau sed 
by numbers or by thinking unverified things are the same, as verified 
ones. We surface this design choice explicitly because we consider 
it a small but genuine methodological contribution: an explicit 
provenance flag on every detection is, in our view,  preferable to 
either silent suppression or silent (and potentially incorrect) 
relabeling, and we recommend it as a general pattern for any 
inspection system built on a checkpoint whose class taxonomy only 
partially overlaps its deployment taxonomy.  
E. A Methodological Finding: Metric Definition Errors  
During iterative development, an internal evaluation utility was 
found to report a quantity labeled "mAP@50" that varied 
continuously with the confidence threshold supplied to it (for 
example, 94.28% at threshold 0.10 rising monotonically to 96.87% 
at threshold 0.35 on a validation split). Standard mAP@50, as 
defined by the precision -recall integral in Eq. (1) -(2) below and as 
computed by the Ultralytics validation API used for every number 
in Tables I -IV threshold -independent is by construction because 
threshold -independent integrates precision over the recall range for 
each class, before averaging:  
AP = ∫₀¹ P(R) dR, mAP  = (1/M) Σ AP(i) (1) -(2) 
A metric that changes materially with a manually supplied 
confidence threshold is, by this definition, not mAP; it is more likely 
a precision/recall/F1 figure computed at a single fixed operating 
point and mislabeled. We flag this not to single out any one  script, 
but because we believe it is a common and under -reported failure 
mode in applied PCB -inspection literature and internal tooling alike: 
any accuracy claim that cites a confidence threshold alongside 
"mAP " merits independent re -verification with the standard, 
threshold -integrated computation before being trusted or published. 
All figures in Tables I -IV were produced only after this re -
verification step.  
VI. DISCUSSION, LIMITATIONS, AND 
IMPROVEMENT PLAN  
A. Honest Limitations  
Four limitations materially qualify the results in this paper. First 
even though the domain -boundary ambiguity mentioned in Section 
V-C was mostly cleared up for the defect detector through retraining 
from scratch the 98.3% result from the legacy checkpoin t is still 
listed as a disclosed but not adopted data point. That’s because we 
were unable to confirm what exactly made up the external training 
data used for it. Second the explainability module relies on an 
activation -energy heuristic, not on gradient -based methods, like 
Grad -CAM or Grad -CAM++. Also  its ability to provide attributions 
hasn’t been tested using insertion or deletion experiments nor has it 
been evaluated with pointing -game protocols. Third, the component 
detector remains weak (Section V -B) and its own from -scratch 
retraining attempt was  invalidated by a configuration error rather 
than completed, so the platform's inspection workload is currently 
carried almost entirely by the defect detector; component 
identification should be considered auxiliary and low -reliability 
pending a corrected retraining run. Fourth, all training and 
evaluation in this environment was CPU -bound except for the two 
from -scratch training runs (successful for the defect detector, 
invalidated for the component detector) which were executed on a 
cloud GPU notebook; th is mixed compute environment constrained 
the number of independent training repetitions available for 
reporting mean -and-variance statistics of the kind Ye et al. [3] 
provide via five repeated runs, so single -run figures should be read 
with this in mind.  
B. Concrete Improvement Plan  
Guided directly by the ablation results of Ye et al. [3] and by the 
defect detector's successful from -scratch resolution, we propose the 
following prioritized, individually -ablatable  improvements for the 
next development cycle, focused primarily on the component

detector. (1) Re -execute the from -scratch component training with 
explicit, verified dataset -path configuration, correcting the error 
identified in Section V -B, and validate the resulting checkpoint 
locally immediately after training rather than trusting t he remote 
job's own reported metrics uncritically. (2) Acquire the PKU -
Market -PCB dataset to extend the defect detector's already -strong

currently unavailable in this environment. (3) Intr oduce the 
C3k2_Faster_EMA neck design ( FasterNet partial convolution plus 
efficient multi -scale attention) in place of standard C3k2 blocks, 
which the base paper shows recovers accuracy lost to backbone 
lightweighting while further reducing parameter count. (4) Replace 
the CIoU bounding -box los s with the combined Inner -IoU/Wise -
IoU_v3 loss, sweeping the Inner -IoU ratio parameter on validation 
data rather than assuming the base paper's reported optimum (0.90) 
transfers directly to our data distribution. (5) For the component 
detector retrain using the tiled high -resolution pipeline that was 
validated qualitatively in Section IV -B. Use a corrected annotation 
set that includes only the six classes and update the evaluation split 
to reduce the overrepresentation of board -level data. Currently one 
single board makes up 64% of the test images, which skews results. 
This c hange aims to balance the dataset. (6) Apply test -time 
augmentation during inference. If computational resources allow, 
also use weighted -box-fusion ensembling across training seeds. 
These techniques should be used as accuracy boosters. Reported 
separately —each method clearly identified and not combined into a 
single performance number.Each  of these six changes should be 
implemented incrementally. For each one. Report the before -and-
after delta. Follow the ablation table structure from the base paper. 
Maintain the discipline already shown for the defect detector. This 
ensures every future ac curacy claim can be traced back, to a 
reproducible change.  
VII. CONCLUSION  
This paper presented SmartPCB -XAI, a dual -detector, explainable 
PCB inspection platform, and — more centrally — a case study in 
resolving, rather than merely disclosing, a real data -provenance 
ambiguity encountered during development. An externally -trained 
defect -detection checkpoint produced two conflicting accuracy 
readings (91.47% -92.74% under one evaluator, 98.3% under 
another) alongside an admitted, unverifiable contamination risk; 
rather than adopting either figure or leaving the question open, we 
retrained the detector from scra tch on a locally -audited, leakage -
verified split with a fixed seed, obtaining mAP@50 = 95.1% 
(mAP@50 -95 = 63.1%, precision = 93.1%, recall = 90.6%) with no 
contamination caveat required — a result that exceeds both the 
92.3% reported by a recent state -of-the-art lightweight -YOLOv11s 
baseline [3] and our own pre -registered 93% target, despite using 
none of that work's backbone, neck, or loss modifications. Our 
component detector, evaluated with the same rigor, remains 
substantially weaker at 9.3% mAP@50; a p arallel from -scratch 
retraining attempt for this detector was invalidated by a dataset -
configuration error and its output discarded in full rather than 
reported, which we present as a further, deliberate illustration of this 
paper's central methodological argument: an unresolved or invalid 
result should be labeled and, where necessary, discarded, not 
smoothed over.  Future work will pursue the six -point improvement 
plan of Section VI -B, prioritizing a corrected from -scratch 
component retraining, toward closing the remaining gap between the 
platform's two detectors with the same evidentiary standard already 
met for def ect detection.
